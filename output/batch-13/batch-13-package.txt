The scar is shaped like a star — five uneven points, raised and pale against brown skin, sitting on the inside of Megan's left forearm like a brand she never asked for. She was four years old when the fire took the duplex on Parsons Avenue. She doesn't remember flames. She remembers a hand pulling her through a doorframe, and the sound of screaming that wasn't hers, and then nothing for a long time.

The foster system gave her a new last name, a new city for a while, and eventually a job at Rosie's Diner on East Main where the coffee is always burnt and the tips are decent on weekends. Twenty-four years. She's never found the other half of that night.

Until six Tuesdays ago.

Elena Byrne comes in every Tuesday at 9:15. Straight dark hair, olive cardigan, white tee underneath. She orders two eggs over easy, wheat toast, black coffee with one sugar. She reads the paper. She tips 25 percent. And six weeks ago she pushed up her right sleeve to stir that sugar and Megan saw it — five uneven points, raised and pale, sitting on the inside of a forearm in the exact mirror position of her own.

Megan almost dropped the coffee pot that first morning. She went to the back, pressed her forehead against the walk-in cooler door, and breathed until her hands stopped shaking. She told herself it was nothing. Burn scars happen. Stars happen. Coincidences happen.

But she came back the next Tuesday. And the next. And every time Elena pushed up that sleeve, the scar was still there — same size, same shape, same place where a four-year-old's arm would have pressed against a burning doorframe while someone pulled her through.

This morning is Tuesday number seven. Megan stands at booth four with the glass pot and her hands are trembling again. Elena sits with her forearm on the Formica, scar catching the light from the window blinds, completely unaware. She's looking out at the parking lot, stirring her sugar slowly.

Megan pours. The coffee rises in the mug — too high, almost to the rim. She can't look away from Elena's arm. The mark is identical. Not similar. Identical. Five points. The same slight hook on the bottom left ray where the skin folded during healing.

She sets the pot down on the table. Her fingers move to her own left cuff. She hasn't rolled this sleeve up at work in three years. Not since a customer asked about it and she had to make up a story about a cooking accident because the truth — "I was in a fire when I was four and I think my sister was in there too but I've never found her" — isn't something you say to a stranger over pancakes.

But Elena isn't a stranger. Not really. Not if what Megan thinks is true.

She rolls the sleeve up. Slowly. Past the wrist, past the first two inches of unmarked skin, until the star sits exposed under the fluorescent light. She holds her arm out, forearm up, parallel to Elena's on the table.

Elena is still looking out the window. Still stirring.

"Excuse me," Megan says. Her voice comes out smaller than she means it to.

Elena turns. Her eyes go to Megan's face first — polite, ready to order something else — and then drop to the arm held out in front of her. The forearm. The scar.

The spoon stops moving.

Elena's gaze snaps to her own arm. Then back to Megan's. Her lips part but nothing comes out. The color drains from her face in a single wave, forehead to chin, and she grips the edge of the table with both hands.

"Where did you get that?" Elena whispers.

"Parsons Avenue," Megan says. "2002. A duplex fire. I was four."

Elena's hand goes to her mouth. Her eyes fill so fast that tears spill before she can blink. She's shaking her head — not no, but the kind of shaking that means the world just tilted and she's trying to stay upright.

"I was four too," Elena says through her fingers. "They told me there was another girl. They told me she didn't — they said she didn't make it."

Megan's knees go soft. She reaches for the booth's backrest and holds on. The diner sounds — the grill hissing, the bell over the door, someone laughing three tables away — all of it drops to silence.

"I made it," Megan says.

Elena stands up so fast the coffee mug rocks. She grabs Megan's forearm — gently, both hands wrapped around the scar like she's holding something that might disappear — and pulls it next to her own. Side by side. Mirror images. The same five points. The same hook on the bottom left ray.

"Oh my God," Elena says. "Oh my God. Oh my God."

She pulls Megan into a hug so hard the coffee pot clatters against the table. Megan's arms come up around her and she can feel Elena shaking — full-body tremors, the kind that come from twenty-four years of grief breaking open all at once.

Rosie herself comes out from behind the register. "Everything okay, girls?"

Neither of them can answer. Elena is crying into Megan's shoulder, hands fisted in the back of her teal polo, and Megan is crying too — silently, eyes closed, holding on like the fire is still burning and this time she's not letting go.

They sit in booth four for two more hours. The eggs go cold. The coffee goes cold. Elena tells Megan about the foster family in Toledo, the adoption at six, the parents who told her the other girl in the fire didn't survive. Megan tells Elena about Columbus, about Rosie's, about the scar she's hidden for two decades because no one ever believed her when she said there was someone else in that hallway.

Elena pulls up her adoption file on her phone. The address listed for the original placement: Parsons Avenue. The fire date: March 14, 2002. Two children recovered. One placed with the Byrne family in Toledo. One placed with emergency foster care in Columbus.

Two girls. Same fire. Same doorframe. Same star.

Three weeks later, the DNA results come back from a lab on Broad Street. Megan drives to Elena's apartment with the envelope unopened. They sit on Elena's couch and open it together.

Full siblings. Sisters.

Elena frames both hospital bracelets — hers and the one Megan's foster mother kept in a shoebox — side by side in a shadow box that now hangs above her kitchen table. The bracelets are twenty-four years old, plastic faded to pale yellow, but the names are still legible: Baby Girl Torres A. Baby Girl Torres B.

Megan still works Tuesdays at Rosie's. Elena still comes in at 9:15. But now she sits at the counter instead of booth four, and Megan pours her coffee with her left sleeve rolled up, the star catching the light like it was always meant to be seen.

The first Tuesday after the DNA results, Elena brought a second mug from home — a ceramic one with a faded star painted on the side, something she'd made in a pottery class years ago without knowing why she chose that shape.

She sets it on the counter every week now. Megan fills it without asking.

Some things find their way back. Even through fire. Even through twenty-four years of silence. Sometimes the clue is as small as a scar shaped like a star, catching the morning light on a stranger's arm in a diner in Columbus, Ohio.

Two weeks later, the estate lawyers confirmed everything. The bond was Claire's sole property — purchased legally, registered to her name, held by a third party per Margaret Aldridge's written instruction. Richard filed a challenge. It was dismissed in four days.

Claire used part of the money to buy a small house in Milford — thirty minutes from Greenwich, a lifetime away from the family she no longer needed to answer to. She planted a garden in the backyard. Installed a wrought-iron bench.

Every Christmas morning, she brings two thermoses of coffee to Evergreen Hills. She sits with Marcus on the bench under the old elm — the same bench where her mother sat for thirty years — and they talk until the coffee goes cold.

The crumpled dollar bill still sits on Margaret Aldridge's grave border. Marcus never moved it. Neither did Richard. It stays there through rain and snow and the slow turn of seasons, curling at the edges, fading in the sun — a one-dollar tip on a $1.2 million truth.

"Find me when you're ready. I'll smell like golden flowers." The note was written on lined paper torn from a spiral notebook, the edges still ragged. Danielle found it in her foster file when she turned eighteen — tucked between a birth certificate and a court order that terminated parental rights. The handwriting was loopy, hurried, the pen pressed so hard it left grooves on the back of the page. Signed: Patricia.

Danielle kept the note in her wallet for fourteen years. She carried it through nursing school, through her marriage to David, through two miscarriages and one promotion and a thousand night shifts at St. Thomas West ER in Nashville. She never looked for Patricia. Looking meant hoping, and hoping meant being four years old again, sitting on a porch with a garbage bag of clothes, watching a car pull away and not come back.

But she never forgot the scent. Shalimar. Amber and vanilla and something like powdered flowers. The smell of being held in the dark by someone warm, being told it would be okay, and then waking up to someone else entirely.

Amber. Vanilla. Sandalwood. Golden flowers.

Danielle's knees buckle. She grabs the curtain rail and holds herself upright. Patricia's eyes drift closed again. Her breathing steadies. She doesn't know anyone heard.

Danielle sits in the break room for forty minutes. She doesn't cry. She doesn't call David. She pulls the note from her wallet — worn soft at the folds, the ink fading but still legible — and reads it for the ten-thousandth time. "Find me when you're ready. I'll smell like golden flowers."

Every Thursday night, Danielle brings two coffees to Patricia's apartment before her shift. They sit at the kitchen table and talk — twenty-eight years of talking, parceled out in hour-long visits because there's no way to do it all at once. Sometimes Patricia cries. Sometimes Danielle does. Mostly they just sit close enough to smell the Shalimar and know that the distance is finally, permanently, closed.

The amber bottle with the gold cap sits on Patricia's bedside table now. She's worn the same perfume for thirty-two years. She was right.
