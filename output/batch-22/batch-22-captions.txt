M001 | Forgotten Birthdays → A Drawer Full of Love
---
She never once remembered a birthday. Not one card. Not a single phone call on the day that mattered. For eighteen years, Mia Torres kept count — kept score, really — and by the time she was twenty-six, she'd stopped expecting anything from her Aunt Dolores altogether. When the call came asking for help packing up the apartment before the move to assisted living, Mia said yes out of obligation, not love. She showed up with empty boxes and a tight jaw.

The apartment was smaller than she remembered. Yellowed curtains filtered the late-afternoon sun into something almost golden, dust motes spinning in the light above cluttered shelves. Dolores sat alone at the kitchen table, stirring tea with swollen knuckles, not even looking up when Mia started pulling shoeboxes from the bedroom closet. And then Mia noticed the dresser — heavy, old, shoved against the far wall. One bottom drawer had a brass lock she'd never seen before. She tugged at it. Locked. She tugged harder.

The lock gave. The drawer slid open. Inside: eighteen individually wrapped packages. Small ones, careful ones, each tagged with a year and a name — her name. A doll. A journal. A bracelet. A graduation watch. Mia's hands went still. Behind the gifts, a stack of hospital bills and pay stubs from a second job she never knew existed. And at the very bottom, a note in shaking handwriting.

He wore the same faded brown corduroy jacket with hand-sewn elbow patches year after year. He chipped in exactly five dollars for group gifts. Never a cent more.

He nodded once and said nothing. And then, fourteen days before the deadline, a cashier's check arrived — no name, no note, the exact remaining balance down to the cent. When she traced it, the account led to one name. And inside his apartment, in a shoebox on the closet shelf, she found a ledger with seventeen years of entries.

M005 | Two Cups at the Booth → Forty-One Years of Anniversaries

For forty-one years, every October 14th, he orders two cups of coffee.

One black. One with cream and two sugars. Harold Meeks slides into the same corner booth at Rosie's Diner in Asheville before the morning rush — brown wool cardigan buttoned over a pressed flannel shirt, gold wedding band worn smooth to nothing on his left hand — and he pushes the second cup across the checkered tablecloth to the seat nobody's occupied in over two decades. The steam curls up from the untouched mug. The sugar dispenser sits beside it, undisturbed. He wraps both weathered hands around his own cup and watches the empty vinyl seat across from him like it might say something back.

Jenna started waitressing at Rosie's three months ago. She noticed him on her first October shift — how he arrives at exactly 7:15, sits for precisely one hour, stirs the second cup once at the thirty-minute mark without lifting it, then leaves a twenty-dollar tip on a six-dollar tab. She asked Rosie about it. Rosie shrugged. "He's done that since before I bought the place." No one had ever asked him directly. No one seemed to think they should.

This morning, Jenna refilled his black coffee without being asked. He looked up — just briefly — and his eyes caught the early light through the window the same way the steam caught it. She almost spoke. He almost let her. And then he turned back to the empty seat, and stirred the second cup right on schedule.

M009 | A Candle Every Dusk → The Crew That Never Came Home
---
For thirty-four years, every evening at dusk, she lights the candle.

Miriam Foley stands at the same salt-weathered window in her clapboard house above Gloucester Harbor — white hair in a loose braid, cable-knit cream sweater, gold locket at her throat — and cups one weathered hand around a white pillar candle on the wide sill until the flame holds steady. Through the glass, the harbor stretches dark and empty. No boats at the docks tonight. No boats most nights, not anymore. The windowsill paint is cracked and peeling from decades of salt air. A box of matches sits beside the candle, always in the same spot, always within reach.

She stands there for exactly thirty seconds after the flame catches — hand on the glass, eyes toward the water — and then she turns away. Every dusk. Her neighbors call her eccentric. The new man next door — young, dark curly hair, marine-lab shirt — watches from his porch sometimes with a kind of quiet curiosity, but he hasn't asked yet.

Tonight the sky behind the harbor is deep orange bleeding into purple. The candle flame reflects in the dark glass, doubling itself. Inside, the room is spare: one armchair, one side table, a framed photograph lying face-down. Miriam's locket catches the candlelight as she straightens. She doesn't look at the photograph. She hasn't turned it face-up in years. But the candle — the candle she never forgets.
